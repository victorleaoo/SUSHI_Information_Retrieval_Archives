Each task provides a collection context, an information need, and a list of available subject-numeric codes and headings. Predict the filing categories and retrieval terms most likely to lead to records responsive to the need.

Records were generally filed according to the creator’s responsibilities and the work being done, so relevant material may be under a related program, office function, actor, or transaction rather than a heading that repeats the researcher’s wording.

First identify the central issue, people or organizations, place, period, and activity sought. Then consider which kinds of work could have generated responsive records: for example, policy or law, program administration, reporting and statistics, official contacts or negotiations, individual casework, assistance, or institutional affairs. Use only pathways that fit the request and the collection context. Consider which office or sector would most likely have handled the matter; distinguish, where relevant, between concerns involving people, animals, institutions, territory, commerce, or other domains.

Select a small set of plausible, complementary filing routes. Include a broader or general category when records could reasonably have been filed there, not only a narrowly specialized subdivision. When the request could generate distinct kinds of records, consider separate routes for those functions—for example, substantive subject files as well as reporting, legal, diplomatic, or administrative files. Do not choose a code merely because its label shares a word with the request. Do not assume that every plausible route appears in the supplied list, and never invent a code.

For subject_terms, provide focused retrieval language that may help find records despite differences in wording. Include the request’s core concepts, useful synonyms and spelling variants, historically appropriate terminology, and relevant local-language equivalents. Where useful, add likely names of places, people, organizations, programs, or events, along with discriminative process or document terms such as reporting, correspondence, negotiations, legislation, or case files. Use the dates and geography in the context to guide terminology and entities. Avoid long lists of generic associations, speculative names, and modern terms unlikely to have been used in the records.

Return only a manageable set of the most plausible codes, using exact identifiers from the supplied list, and useful search terms. Do not claim that any record definitely exists. Output only valid JSON in this form:
